The framework

Semperism

The name is taken from Virginia’s motto, sic semper tyrannis — thus always to tyrants. The word that matters is semper. The founding settlement was not a truce with power, to be renegotiated each generation. It was a permanent textual prohibition on power exceeding its grant.

The claim

Constitutions fail less often by usurpation than by consecration. An office, a symbol or an institution is first marked out by a sign, then individuated, then revered, and finally consecrated — placed beyond the ordinary reach of the text that created it. Each step feels like respect. The last step is the failure.

Idolatry binds you to the actor through its symbol; the oath binds you to the law that binds the actor. The first you worship. The second you defend. A republic survives on the second and dies of the first.

The word

History calls them Anti-Federalists, a label their opponents gave them that says only what they were against. Mason, who refused to sign at Philadelphia; Henry, who fought ratification in Virginia; the essayist Brutus; Monroe, who voted no and later became President. What they shared was a conviction the old vocabulary never named: some things are always a threat. Consolidation, consecration, the marking of dissent as disloyalty — these are not accidents of bad rulers. They are the always of government, and they call for permanent, written prohibition.

I call that disposition Semperist. It is an interpretive name, not a claimed historical identity. It comes from the motto George Wythe’s committee set on Virginia’s seal in 1776, sic semper tyrannis. John Wilkes Booth later turned the phrase into an assassin’s line. It deserves to be heard again as the founders heard it: not vengeance, but a permanent posture toward power. The seal shows it — Virtus standing over the fallen tyrant, spear grounded, sword sheathed.

Federalists and Semperists

The Federalists believed structure would suffice: ambition set against ambition, because men are not angels. Hamilton argued in Federalist No. 84 that a bill of rights was unnecessary. The Semperists answered that structure without written prohibition invites the very drift structure is meant to slow.

Neither side simply won. Madison drafted the Bill of Rights himself — the Semperist program, enacted by its ablest opponent. Federalist architecture without Semperist prohibition would have been a machine without a cage. Semperist prohibition without Federalist architecture would have been a cage without a machine. The founding succeeded because two rival virtues corrected each other. It took 278 days to say yes to a government, and 1,550 days to say what it must not do.

Anti-consecration architecture

On this reading the Constitution fuses Federalist structural design with a Semperist permanent prohibition: no actor and no institution is placed beyond constraint. The officer’s oath under Article VI runs to the Constitution, not to a person, an office or a flag. The Preamble limits the government’s ends; the Bill of Rights limits its means. A purpose statement is as confining as a rights statement.

Where the work goes from here

The same question — how institutions come to exceed the terms on which they were founded — runs through the papers in the library: the Preamble and the oath, the Bill of Rights and the words it depends on, marriage and the state, displacement and the regimes that cause it, and the reach of institutions over lawful private life.
